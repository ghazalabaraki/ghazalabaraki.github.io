Case 01 | Commercial HVAC | Canada

The owner who couldn't be on every roof

Technicians working alone across sixteen service sectors. No way to know a job was done properly until something went wrong. Four client goals, delivered as one product in 8 sprints.

01

It started when

The owner of a commercial HVAC business couldn't be everywhere his technicians were.

His people worked alone, on rooftops and in mechanical rooms across sixteen service sectors. He had no way of knowing whether a job was being done properly until something went wrong, and by then it was a callback, a warranty claim, or a lost client. Training material existed as videos nobody had time to watch. Scheduling and dispatch lived in their existing scheduling system, a system that didn't talk to anything a technician carried into the field.

He didn't ask for surveillance. He asked for visibility: a way to supervise quality without standing on site.

The brief came in four parts.

GOAL 01

A chatbot on their own data

Not a general purpose assistant. One that answers from the company's own training material and procedures.

GOAL 02

One unified application

All of it in a single place, rather than another tool added to the stack.

GOAL 03

the academy

Structured training the business could assign and track.

GOAL 04

Job tracking

Visibility into work as it happened, in the field.

Underneath those four asks, three things were actually broken.

  • One person was the company's 24/7 help desk. Every technician question, every training request, routed through a single individual, which meant the business could not grow faster than one person's availability
  • The knowledge existed but was scattered: screen recordings, unstructured documents, and whatever managers happened to carry in their heads
  • Nothing was measured. No record of who had been trained, how technicians were performing, or whether procedures were being followed

That is the difference between a feature request and a problem statement. The client asked for a chatbot. What they needed was for expertise to stop living in one person and start living in a system.

02

The idea that made it work

The checklist wasn't a feature. It was the instrument.

Breaking every job into discrete, verifiable steps meant the technician got guidance and the owner got remote visibility from the same interaction. One mechanism, two problems solved. A technician working through a checklist isn't being monitored, they're being supported, and the supervision comes free as a by-product of the support.

That framing also solved the hardest part of the scope. Four goals delivered as four modules would have been a much easier build. Delivering them as one product meant the assistant, the academy and job tracking all had to share a single model of what a technician, a job, and a piece of training content are.

03

What I owned

  • Product end to end: discovery, scope, roadmap, backlog and release sequencing across three surfaces delivered in parallel
  • Translated a four part brief into one coherent product rather than four bolted together modules
  • Structured the job workflow as step by step checklists tied to shift check in, so quality assurance and remote supervision came from the same flow the technician was already in
  • Scoped the training content pipeline around where the client's material already lived, so nobody had to migrate content before seeing value
  • Specified what the pipeline generates automatically from that content: summaries, checklists, quizzes, reading material and assessments, each replacing something a supervisor previously made by hand
  • Managed the their existing scheduling system integration as a dependency with its own risk profile: weekly job synchronisation, admin calendar and dispatch views, sixteen sector route lookup, truck management
  • Defined roles and permissions for the academy: hierarchical courses, role based assignment, progress tracking, and admin regeneration when source material changed
  • Ran QA and release readiness gates before anything reached the client
  • Owned client communication kickoff to post launch: pre milestone check ins, written weekly updates, scope changes negotiated in writing at decision maker level

04

Decisions and trade-offs

Build the academy before the assistant

The callShipped in this order: the academy, then the assistant, then job tracking and checklists, then the their existing scheduling system integration. WhyThe assistant answers from the company's own training material, which means that material has to exist, be structured and be ingestible before the assistant has anything to answer from. Building the bot first would have meant demoing it against nothing. The academy wasn't just the first deliverable, it was the dependency the assistant stood on. ThenJob tracking followed once technicians had a reason to be in the app daily. The their existing scheduling system integration went last: the only piece dependent on a system outside the team's control, so it carried the highest schedule risk and the least coupling. Sequencing it last kept an external dependency off the critical path.

Ship a mobile web client, not a native app

The callBuild the technician client as a mobile optimised web app rather than native iOS and Android. WhyThe fastest route to something the client could put in a technician's hands and react to. A testable build in weeks meant real feedback shaping the product while there was still time to act on it, instead of a longer native build delivering its first honest feedback after the MVP window had closed. Traded awayNative capabilities, including reliable offline use, and technicians work in mechanical rooms and basements without signal. Accepted for the MVP, on the basis that learning what the product needed to be mattered more than polish at that stage.

Integrate their existing scheduling system rather than rebuild scheduling

The callPull scheduling, dispatch and job data from their existing scheduling system instead of building a scheduling system inside the platform. WhyThe client's live business already ran on it. Their jobs, routes and truck assignments existed there and would keep existing there. A parallel system would have meant double entry and two versions of the truth. The only way the platform could show real job progress was to read from the system where real jobs already lived. Traded awayControl. The integration inherits their existing scheduling system's data model, its limits and its downtime, and weekly synchronisation set the floor on how fresh the data could be.

One technician role in the MVP, everything else written down as later

The callThe MVP covered air duct cleaning technicians only: their procedures, tools, safety steps, and the full cleaning process end to end. Hiring assessments, onboarding automation, customer service coaching, quality-control review of job photos, supervisor and manager support, and CRM integration all went into a written future-scope section instead. WhyThe assistant's value comes from depth of correct procedure, not breadth of coverage. One role answered accurately is provable, and a technician can act on it on a roof. Five roles answered approximately is a demo that fails the first time someone relies on it. Traded awayAn MVP that sounds smaller than the client's ambition, which covered the whole company. Writing the deferred scope out in full is what made that acceptable: nothing was refused, it was sequenced, and the client could see exactly where their other ideas had gone.

06

What I'd do differently

Understand the business first. Propose the application second.

A client arrives describing what they want built, and it is easy to treat that description as the brief. Four goals arrived, so four goals got scoped. But a client is an expert in their own business and not in software, and what they ask for is their best guess at a solution, phrased in the only vocabulary available to them. Accepting it as the question means never testing whether it is the right question.

What I'd change

Next timeSpend the first weeks inside the business rather than inside the brief. Go out on jobs. Sit with the person fielding technician calls all day. Find out what actually causes a callback, and what a good technician does that a new one doesn't. WhyAn application is an answer. You can only judge whether it is a good one against a problem you understand independently of how the client described it. Everything I got right here came from the moment I stopped treating the brief as four features and started asking why the owner couldn't leave the roof. The tellThe strongest part of this project is the checklist: one mechanism that gives a technician guidance and an owner visibility at the same time. That didn't come from the brief. It came from understanding what the business actually needed. I'd rather find that in week one, by design, than partway through by instinct.
