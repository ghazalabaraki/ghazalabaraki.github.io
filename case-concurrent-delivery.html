Case 03 | AI & digital platforms | Malaysia

Four products, one client, all at once

Most delivery problems are sequencing problems wearing a disguise. Four concurrent products for a single Malaysian client meant every week was a decision about whose dependency moved first.

The four

01

National science platform

A national platform where the public contributes scientific data. Members of the public join missions, photograph a polluted river or log a species, and AI checks each image is actually relevant before it enters the dataset. Points earned are redeemable for physical and digital rewards.

02

On-premise AI workspace

An on-premise AI workspace for organisations that can't send their data to a public model. Multi-model chat, document and spreadsheet analysis, generated charts and exportable reports, with admin controls over how it's used.

03

AI teaching platform

An LLM teaching platform. Teachers generate slides, quizzes and study guides from their own uploaded material. Students talk to an AI tutor while a mindmap of the conversation builds in real time beside them, and the system flags what they struggled with.

04

Court transcription tool

An application for transcribing court proceedings, turning spoken record into searchable text.

02

It started when

Four products, four sets of stakeholders, one delivery calendar.

Each product had a different client conversation, a different definition of done and, usually, a single engineer. A government science ministry wanted a platform the public could be trusted with. An enterprise client wanted AI that never left their own servers. A school platform and a court transcription tool ran alongside both. None of them cared what the other three needed.

The scarce resource wasn't engineering time. It was decision bandwidth. Four products generate four streams of unanswered questions, and unanswered questions are where delivery quietly stops: an engineer picks the interpretation that seems reasonable, builds it, and the mismatch surfaces at review when it is expensive. Everything I did here was designed around that single failure mode.

03

What I owned

  • Owned priorities, delivery, dependencies and stakeholder alignment across all four products simultaneously
  • Took each product from requirements and UI/UX through QA to stakeholder approved release
  • Held the trade-offs between four product lines competing for the same team and the same weeks
  • Ran client communication across a timezone gap, with written updates and pre milestone check ins
  • Wrote the sprint plans: scope, task breakdowns, owners, effort, priority and acceptance criteria for every item
  • Ran root-cause analysis on production defects and wrote the fix as a sequenced plan, not a ticket
  • Tracked the decisions that blocked development, with a named owner against each one

    Decisions and trade-offs

    Nothing gets built on an unmade decision

    The callWhen a task depended on a decision nobody had made, it did not enter the sprint. It went into an open-items table with the decision, why it blocked the work, and the name of the person who owed the answer. ExampleA file retention policy could not be built until someone chose between auto-delete, a quota warning, or a configurable model. Rather than let an engineer pick, the task sat visible and unstarted with two named owners against it. Separately, an AI moderation feature was held until the client had approved a written proposal covering the scoring model and what counted as sensitive content. Traded awayVelocity that looks good on a burndown. A blocked task is an uncomfortable thing to show a client. It is far less uncomfortable than a feature built on a guess and thrown away after review, and naming the owner is what converts a complaint into a decision with a date.

    Defer scope in writing, never quietly

    The callCut scope had its own documented section in every plan, carrying the same detail as delivered work. ExampleOn the classroom platform, five phases were scoped and three delivered. The narrative-learning and gamification phases were deferred and written up in full, deliverable by deliverable, so the client could see exactly what they were not getting yet. The same applied to pilot-readiness work on the citizen science platform. Traded awayA sprint that reads thinner at first glance, in exchange for a roadmap nobody has to reconstruct from memory. Deferred work that isn't written down does not get deprioritised. It gets forgotten, and then it reappears as a surprise commitment.

    Write the diagnosis, not the ticket

    The callDefects went into the plan with a root cause established first, then a sequenced fix and a named file to verify against. ExampleSpreadsheet-driven charts were returning wrong figures. The report did not say "charts are broken." It said the context window passed to the model was too small, so the file was being cut off mid-read and rows were lost. The fix was four ordered steps: enlarge the context, split extraction from rendering into two prompts so the model stopped doing both at once, add tooltips, legends and a fallback state, then verify against the exact file that failed in the demo. Why it matters"It's broken" starts a debugging session. A root cause plus an ordered fix starts the work. A second defect, raw HTML appearing in chat when a user switched models mid-session, was traced the same way: missing sanitisation at the switch, not a rendering bug.

    Every task carries its own acceptance criteria

    The ruleNo task entered a sprint without written acceptance criteria, agreed before work started. Alongside them, a shared vocabulary: effort as XS to L, priority as P1 to P3, with P3 defined openly as valuable work that is allowed to slip. WhyAcross four products, "done" drifts. Written criteria mean the test is a document rather than a conversation at review, and a defined scale means an estimate carries the same meaning on every product rather than being renegotiated each time. The effectContention stopped being a debate. When two products wanted the same week, the priority tier answered it, and I only had to arbitrate the cases where both were genuinely P1.

    06

    What I'd do differently

    Bring in a QA before the third product, not after the fourth.

    Four products on a weekly release cadence, and no dedicated QA function. I wrote acceptance criteria for every task, which is the right control. But the person checking each build against those criteria was largely the same person who had written them and set the priorities. That is one pair of hands holding scope, sequencing and verification for four products at once.

    What it actually cost

    The symptomDefects that should have been caught internally arrived as client feedback instead. An AI validation feature was accepting images it was built to reject, and that reached me through a customer feedback channel rather than through a test pass. A later sprint opened with a task to verify whether an already-shipped constraint still worked, because nobody could say for certain. The real tellUncertainty about the state of shipped functionality. Not knowing is the shape an absent QA function takes, and it costs more than the bugs themselves, because it turns every release into a question. Next timeArgue for a dedicated QA at the point the second product is committed, and make the case in delivery terms rather than headcount terms: this is what we currently pay in rework and client-found defects, and this is what it costs to stop paying it.

    There's a thread here that runs back through my own work. On an earlier product I held product, design and QA at once, and I compensated with a rule: acceptance criteria written down before any design started, then tested against that written record rather than against memory. That rule is sound and it worked. What I learned here is that it doesn't scale. At one product the constraint is my objectivity, and a rule can fix that. At four, the constraint is my hours, and no rule fixes that. A control that depends on one person's time has a ceiling, and this is where I found mine.
