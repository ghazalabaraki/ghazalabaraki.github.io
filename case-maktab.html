01

It started when

Schools were running on paper registers. Every student record, every fee, every calculation.

Student data lived in physical registers on a shelf. Finances were worked out by hand from those same registers, which meant fee collection, dues and expenses were only as accurate as whoever last added them up. There was no single place to look at a school and see how it was actually doing.

The problem wasn't that schools lacked software. It was that everything a school knows about itself sat in books nobody could query, reconcile or trust. Digitising the records mattered less than putting them all in one place.

02

Wearing three hats

On this one I was product manager, UI/UX and QA at the same time.

That's a real constraint, not a boast. Every hour spent designing a flow was an hour not spent testing one, and the person deciding what to build was also the person deciding whether it was good enough to ship. It makes a small team fast, and it removes the check that normally catches a product manager's blind spots.

The rule that kept me honest

The problemWhen you write the spec, design the screen and then test it, you test against what you meant rather than what you wrote. You pass your own work because you already know how it's supposed to behave. The ruleSeparate the roles in time, not just in name. Acceptance criteria written down before any design started, then tested against that written record rather than against memory. Past me checks present me. Why it worksIt turns an opinion into a document. If the build doesn't match what was written before anyone drew a screen, it fails, regardless of whether it feels right in the moment.

03

Decisions and trade-offs

Sequence by who feels it first: students, then admin, then teachers

The callBuild the student side first. Then the admin and finance side. Teachers last. WhyStudent records are the spine. Fees, classes, attendance and reporting all hang off knowing who the students are, so nothing else could be built until that existed. Admin and finance came next because that was where the pain was loudest, hand-calculated fees from paper registers. Teachers last, because their workflows depend on both of the layers underneath being real. Traded awayTeachers waited. They're the most numerous users and the ones asking earliest, and they were the last group to get anything. That's uncomfortable to hold, and it was the right order.

Ship two modules properly rather than five partially

The callPhase one went live with student management and finance only. Digital exams, attendance, parent and teacher apps, school websites and AI insights were all scoped and deliberately deferred. WhyA school can't half-adopt a system. If fees are only partly in the product, the registers stay open and nothing has changed. Two modules that fully replace a paper process beat five that each replace a piece of one. Traded awayA thinner-looking product in early sales conversations, against a roadmap that could be shown honestly rather than a demo that fell apart under questions.

04

What I owned

  • Product lifecycle from concept through to a live subscription revenue launch
  • Roadmap and phasing across five planned stages, with the line drawn at what ships first
  • UI/UX alongside product: flows, screens, and design to build handoff
  • QA and release readiness across 25 sprints, on a weekly release cadence
  • Live customer onboarding, taking real schools onto the platform rather than handing over and walking away
  • Product input into the pitch deck and positioning used to sell it

05

What shipped

Student records, fee management, attendance and financial dashboards, in one place, replacing the registers rather than sitting alongside them.

07

And then it was wound down

The product wasn't the problem. Nobody was selling it.

Maktab shipped, worked, and won paying schools. It was eventually wound down, and the reason wasn't the build. It was that a company product needs demand created for it, and that work never started early enough to matter.

I raised this with management while we were still building: if we want our own product rather than client work, the marketing has to start first and start early. Client projects arrive with a customer already attached. A company product has to go and find its customers, and that is a workstream with its own timeline, not something you bolt on after launch.

What I'd do differently

The mistakeI treated the market as someone else's workstream. I owned discovery, scope, delivery, QA and onboarding, and I flagged the marketing gap, but I never produced the artefact that would have forced the conversation. Next timeWrite the market analysis myself, before writing the roadmap. Sizing, segments, who buys, what they pay, how they'd hear about it, and what has to be true for the product to reach them. Put it beside the delivery plan so both are visible from day one. Why it mattersA roadmap answers what we're building. It doesn't answer who will buy it or how they'll find out. On client work someone else has already answered that. On your own product, if the product manager doesn't ask it, often nobody does.

I stand by the delivery decisions on this project. The one I'd change is the scope of my own role: on a company product, go-to-market belongs inside product management, not next to it.
