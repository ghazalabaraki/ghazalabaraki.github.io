Case 05 | People & operations | Pakistan

Building the people function from zero

First HR hire at a company about to grow five times over. No policies, no handbook, no hiring process, no review cycle. Eighteen months later: 45+ people and the systems holding them together.

01

It started when

A company of eight people decided to become a company of forty-five, and nothing existed to make that survivable.

No employee handbook. No leave policy. No performance framework. No hiring process beyond asking around. At eight people you can run a company on shared context and goodwill, because everyone is in the same room and everyone knows everything. That model breaks somewhere around twenty, quietly, and by the time anyone notices, the damage is already done: inconsistent offers, unclear expectations, and new people who never quite land.

I was hired as the first HR person to build the function before that break happened, not after it.

Eight people in dark. Thirty-seven more added over eighteen months.

02

Why this belongs in a product portfolio

Because it's the same work. A scaling company is a system with users, failure modes and a backlog. Hiring is a funnel with conversion rates. Onboarding is activation. A review cycle is a feedback loop with a cadence. Policies are the documentation layer, and an employee handbook is a product spec for how a company behaves.

Everything I later applied to software delivery, defining scope before building, sequencing by dependency, writing things down so decisions survive the meeting, I learned here first, on a system where the users were people and the bugs were human.

03

What I owned

  • Scaled headcount from 8 to 45+ in 18 months, designing the hiring, onboarding and training processes that made it possible
  • Managed 50+ hires end to end: job descriptions, sourcing, interviews, offers and onboarding
  • Built the HR function from nothing: policy, performance cycles, employee relations and employment law compliance
  • Authored the employee handbook, leave policies and lifecycle templates, the documentation layer a growing company runs on
  • Ran 2 biannual review cycles and 18+ monthly town halls, and handled escalations directly with executives
  • Applied HR analytics to workforce planning and process optimisation
  • Defined the company brand system with the design team: logo, colour palette, guidelines and tone of voice
  • Contributed to executive planning on international expansion

04

Decisions and trade-offs

Write the leave policy, then announce it in the open

The problemPeople were taking holiday and leave without telling anyone. It wasn't defiance. There was no policy to follow, so everyone improvised, and the cost landed on whoever happened to be covering. The callWrite the policy, then introduce it at a town hall in front of everyone, rather than enforcing it quietly manager by manager. Traded awayAnnouncing a new rule publicly means the resistance is public too, and there was resistance. Rolling it out privately would have been more comfortable and far weaker: a policy that arrives through side conversations is a policy people negotiate with. It was adopted slowly, and it held, because everyone heard the same thing at the same time.

Hire against written values, not against a feeling

The callScreen for personal values and curiosity, assessed against the values the company had actually defined and written down, alongside the skills the role needed. WhyWhen a company goes from 8 to 45, the culture is not set by the founders any more, it's set by whoever arrives next. Curiosity in particular, because in a company that size the role someone is hired into is not the role they'll be doing in six months. Traded awaySpeed. Turning down capable people slows a hiring surge, and there is constant pressure to fill the seat in front of you. The check against that was having values written down first, so the bar was a document everyone could see rather than my instinct in the room.

06

What I'd do differently

Build the pipeline instead of refilling it 50 times.

I ran 50+ hires end to end, and sourcing took the largest share of that time. Every role started from a cold start: write the description, go and find people, screen from scratch. It worked, and it did not compound. Eighteen months of hiring left me with 45 colleagues and no faster way to find the 46th.

Partner with universities early

Next timeBuild direct relationships with university departments in the first months, not once hiring is already urgent. Universities know which of their students are outstanding, and they keep the records that prove it. Why it compoundsA standing relationship turns sourcing from a task I repeat into a channel that keeps producing. It reaches candidates before they are on the open market, and it gives back the hours that repeated cold sourcing consumes. The lessonI optimised each hire when I should have been building the system that produces hires. That's the same mistake as shipping features instead of fixing the workflow underneath them, and I'd catch it faster now.
